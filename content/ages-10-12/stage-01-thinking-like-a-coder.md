# Stage 1 Reading Materials: Think Like a Computer

## Course placement

- Audience: ages 10 to 12
- Stage: 1 of the Code Explorers pathway
- Sessions: 8
- Mentor: Fara the Fox
- Badge: Fox Planner
- Main project: Teach a robot to complete an everyday mission
- Independence goal: A child should finish every session without an adult explaining the instructions

## Stage outcome

By the end of this stage, the learner should be able to:

- Explain the difference between a person giving an instruction and a machine carrying it out.
- Identify an input, the work a machine does and the output it produces.
- Turn vague instructions into clear actions.
- Break a large task into smaller jobs.
- Notice a repeating pattern.
- Predict a result before testing.
- Find and repair a missing step.
- Write and test an ordered robot plan.

## Language and interaction rules

- Write directly to the learner using "you."
- Keep each reading section below 90 words.
- Place an activity after no more than three short paragraphs.
- Introduce only one main idea per screen.
- Explain a new word before asking the learner to use it.
- Let the learner attempt each challenge before revealing a hint.
- Give three levels of help: a small clue, a stronger clue and a worked start.
- Never end the lesson after a wrong answer.
- Ask the learner to explain one decision before completion.
- Save progress after every completed activity.

## Stage vocabulary

### Programmer

A person who creates instructions for a computer or machine.

### Program

A set of instructions that tells a computer what to do.

### Input

Information or an action that goes into a computer or machine.

### Work

What the computer or machine does with the input. This is sometimes called processing.

### Output

The result produced by the computer or machine.

### Decomposition

Breaking one large job into smaller jobs that are easier to understand.

### Pattern

Something that repeats in a predictable way.

### Prediction

What you think will happen before you test a plan.

### Test

Running a plan to see what actually happens.

### Bug

A mistake that causes a program to behave differently from what you expected.

### Debug

To find the cause of a problem and make a careful change.

# Session 1: You Are the Programmer

## Child promise

Today you will discover who is really in charge when a machine follows instructions.

## Story mission

Kito the robot is waiting outside the Idea Lab. Kito can move, speak and switch on the lab lights. There is only one problem. Kito will do nothing until someone gives an instruction.

Fara the Fox asks:

"Who makes the choice, and who carries it out?"

## Read: A computer does not choose your goal

A computer can work very quickly, but it does not automatically know what you want.

A person chooses the goal. The person gives instructions. The computer follows those instructions and produces a result.

The person who creates the instructions is the programmer. The instructions together form a program.

When you choose what Kito should do, you are the programmer.

## Worked example

Goal: Make Kito welcome a visitor.

- The person decides that Kito should say hello.
- The instruction is: Say "Welcome to the Idea Lab."
- Kito carries out the instruction.
- The visitor hears the greeting.

The person chose the action. Kito performed it.

## Your turn: Who did what?

For each card, choose Person, Instruction or Machine action.

1. Amina decides that the music should start.
2. playMusic()
3. The tablet plays the song.
4. Kamau chooses a character for his game.
5. moveCharacterRight()
6. The character moves across the screen.

### Correct classification

1. Person
2. Instruction
3. Machine action
4. Person
5. Instruction
6. Machine action

## Hint ladder

### Hint 1

Ask yourself: Is someone making a choice, giving a command or carrying it out?

### Hint 2

A person's name often appears when a decision is being made.

### Hint 3

An instruction tells the machine what to do. A machine action is what happens after that instruction runs.

## Quick check

### Question 1

Who decides the goal of a program?

- A. The screen
- B. The programmer
- C. The battery

Correct answer: B

Feedback: The programmer decides what the program should help the computer do.

### Question 2

Which item is an instruction?

- A. Turn left
- B. The robot turns left
- C. The learner chooses a route

Correct answer: A

Feedback: "Turn left" tells the robot what action to perform.

### Question 3

True or false: A fast computer always knows what the learner wants.

Correct answer: False

Feedback: A computer needs instructions. Speed does not give it the ability to guess your goal.

## Small creation

Choose one machine at home or school. Write:

- One action chosen by a person
- One instruction given to the machine
- One result produced by the machine

Possible machines include a phone, calculator, television, microwave or school bell.

## Explain it yourself

Complete this sentence:

"A programmer is important because..."

## Completion evidence

The learner submits one correct person, instruction and machine-action chain.

# Session 2: Input, Work, Output

## Child promise

Today you will look inside an everyday machine without opening it.

## Story mission

The Idea Lab has a new snack machine. Kito presses a button marked POPCORN. The machine reads the choice, prepares the snack and releases a packet.

Fara asks:

"What went in, what happened inside and what came out?"

## Read: Three parts of a result

Many computer actions can be understood using three parts:

1. Input
2. Work
3. Output

Input is what enters the system. It may be a button press, a typed word, a picture, a sound or a number.

Work is what the machine does with the input.

Output is the result. It may appear as movement, sound, text, light or a changed number.

## Worked example

A calculator solves 7 + 5.

- Input: The learner enters 7 + 5.
- Work: The calculator adds the numbers.
- Output: The screen shows 12.

The answer is not the input. It is the output produced after the calculator does its work.

## Your turn: Build the snack machine

Put these cards under Input, Work or Output:

- The learner taps JUICE.
- The machine reads the selected drink.
- The machine checks whether juice is available.
- A juice box drops into the tray.
- The screen shows "Enjoy your drink."

### Correct arrangement

Input:

- The learner taps JUICE.

Work:

- The machine reads the selected drink.
- The machine checks whether juice is available.

Output:

- A juice box drops into the tray.
- The screen shows "Enjoy your drink."

## Hint ladder

### Hint 1

The input happens first.

### Hint 2

Work usually happens inside the machine.

### Hint 3

The output is what the learner can see, hear or receive at the end.

## Quick check

### Question 1

A learner presses the Space key and a game character jumps. What is the input?

Correct answer: Pressing the Space key

### Question 2

What is the output?

Correct answer: The character jumps

### Question 3

A weather app compares temperature data before showing a message. Is comparing the data input, work or output?

Correct answer: Work

## Small creation

Choose one of these systems:

- A school calculator
- A music player
- A camera
- A quiz game

Write one input, one piece of work and one output for your chosen system.

## Explain it yourself

Finish this sentence:

"An output is different from an input because..."

## Completion evidence

The learner builds one complete Input, Work, Output chain in the correct order.

# Session 3: The Fussy Robot

## Child promise

Today you will turn a vague idea into instructions a robot can actually follow.

## Story mission

Kito has been asked to make a jam sandwich.

The instruction says:

"Make the sandwich."

Kito freezes. Which bread should be used? Where is the jam? Should the jar be opened? How much jam is enough?

Kito is not being difficult. The instruction expects the robot to guess.

## Read: Clear instructions remove guessing

People use experience to fill in missing information. A computer does not share all of that experience.

A useful instruction tells the machine exactly what action to perform.

"Get the bread" is clearer than "Get the food."

"Spread one spoon of jam on one slice" is clearer than "Add some jam."

Precise instructions describe one action at a time and put actions in an order that can be followed.

## Worked example

Vague instruction:

"Put everything together."

Clearer instructions:

1. Place two slices of bread on the plate.
2. Open the jam jar.
3. Put one spoon of jam on the first slice.
4. Spread the jam across the slice.
5. Place the second slice on top.
6. Close the jam jar.

The clearer version gives Kito less to guess.

## Your turn: Repair the instructions

Change each vague instruction into a clearer one.

1. Move over there.
2. Add some water.
3. Press the thing.
4. Wait for a while.

### Example answers

1. Move forward three steps to the table.
2. Pour one cup of water into the bottle.
3. Press the purple START button once.
4. Wait for ten seconds.

Accept other answers when they include a clear action and enough information to carry it out.

## Hint ladder

### Hint 1

Look for words such as there, some, thing and a while.

### Hint 2

Add a number, direction, object or time.

### Hint 3

Use this pattern: Action + object + exact detail.

## Quick check

### Question 1

Which instruction is more precise?

- A. Move a little.
- B. Move forward two squares.

Correct answer: B

### Question 2

Why might "choose a nice colour" confuse a computer?

Correct answer: "Nice" is an opinion and does not identify one exact colour.

### Question 3

Rewrite "play the sound again" so the number of repeats is clear.

Example correct answer: Play the sound two more times.

## Small creation

Write at least six precise instructions that teach Kito to make a simple snack or drink.

Safety note for the learner: Write the plan only. Do not use heat, sharp tools or kitchen equipment without an adult.

## Explain it yourself

Name one word that can make an instruction too vague and explain how you would replace it.

## Completion evidence

The learner repairs four vague instructions and writes a six-step plan.

# Session 4: Break Up a Big Job

## Child promise

Today you will make a large job feel smaller before you begin it.

## Story mission

Kito receives a new instruction:

"Get ready for school."

Kito's task list becomes enormous. Wash, dress, pack, eat, check the time and leave.

Fara suggests splitting the large job into smaller jobs.

## Read: Big jobs can hide many smaller jobs

Decomposition means breaking one large problem into smaller parts.

A smaller job is easier to plan, test and repair.

"Get ready for school" can become:

- Get yourself ready.
- Prepare your bag.
- Eat breakfast.
- Check before leaving.

Each smaller job can then be divided into clear steps.

Programmers use decomposition when an application, game or website is too large to build all at once.

## Worked example

Large job: Prepare for football practice.

Smaller jobs:

1. Pack equipment.
2. Fill the water bottle.
3. Put on the correct clothes.
4. Check the meeting time.

Inside "Pack equipment":

- Put boots in the bag.
- Add socks.
- Add shin guards.
- Close the bag.

## Your turn: Pack for school

Place each step under the correct smaller job.

Smaller jobs:

- Pack learning materials
- Pack food and water
- Check before leaving

Steps:

- Add the mathematics book.
- Fill the water bottle.
- Put homework in the bag.
- Add the lunch box.
- Check the timetable.
- Check that the bag is closed.

### Correct arrangement

Pack learning materials:

- Add the mathematics book.
- Put homework in the bag.

Pack food and water:

- Fill the water bottle.
- Add the lunch box.

Check before leaving:

- Check the timetable.
- Check that the bag is closed.

## Hint ladder

### Hint 1

Find steps that help with the same small goal.

### Hint 2

Books and homework belong together. Food and drinks belong together.

### Hint 3

The final group contains actions that confirm the learner is ready to leave.

## Quick check

### Question 1

What does decomposition mean?

Correct answer: Breaking a large job into smaller jobs.

### Question 2

Why is a smaller job easier to debug?

Correct answer: There are fewer steps to inspect when something goes wrong.

### Question 3

Which is a useful smaller job inside "Plan a birthday party"?

- A. Do everything
- B. Choose food
- C. Have a good party

Correct answer: B

## Small creation

Choose one large task:

- Clean a bedroom
- Prepare for a journey
- Organise a study session
- Set up a board game

Break it into at least three smaller jobs. Add at least two steps inside each job.

## Explain it yourself

Complete this sentence:

"I would use decomposition when..."

## Completion evidence

The learner creates a task map containing at least three smaller jobs and six clear steps.

# Session 5: Find the Pattern

## Child promise

Today you will notice repeated information and predict what comes next.

## Story mission

Fara is decorating the Idea Lab floor with paper tiles.

The colours repeat:

Purple, navy, navy, purple, navy, navy...

Kito needs to place the next four tiles. Instead of guessing, Kito must find the smallest part that repeats.

## Read: A pattern follows a rule

A pattern is an arrangement that repeats or changes according to a rule.

When programmers notice a pattern, they can avoid writing the same instructions again and again.

Start by looking for the smallest group that repeats.

In this pattern:

Purple, navy, navy, purple, navy, navy

the repeating group is:

Purple, navy, navy

Once you know the repeating group, you can predict what comes next.

## Worked examples

Pattern 1:

Clap, jump, clap, jump, clap, jump

Smallest repeating group:

Clap, jump

Next two actions:

Clap, jump

Pattern 2:

2, 4, 6, 8

Rule:

Add 2

Next two numbers:

10, 12

## Your turn: Tile Detective

Pattern:

Purple, navy, navy, purple, navy, navy

Choose the next four colours.

Correct answer:

Purple, navy, navy, purple

## Pattern challenges

### Challenge A

Fox, elephant, fox, elephant, fox, elephant

What comes next?

Correct answer: Fox

### Challenge B

1, 3, 5, 7

What comes next?

Correct answer: 9

### Challenge C

Turn, move, move, turn, move, move

What is the smallest repeating group?

Correct answer: Turn, move, move

## Hint ladder

### Hint 1

Compare the first item with later items.

### Hint 2

Draw a line after each group that looks the same.

### Hint 3

For the tile pattern, make groups of three.

## Quick check

### Question 1

What is the smallest repeating group in A, B, B, A, B, B?

Correct answer: A, B, B

### Question 2

What comes next in 5, 10, 15, 20?

Correct answer: 25

### Question 3

Why do programmers look for patterns?

Correct answer: Patterns can help them reuse instructions and solve repeated work more efficiently.

## Small creation

Create your own repeating pattern using colours, animals, sounds, movements or numbers.

Requirements:

- Use a repeating group containing at least three items.
- Repeat the group at least three times.
- Hide the final item and ask the computer or another learner to predict it.

## Explain it yourself

State the rule behind your pattern.

## Completion evidence

The learner identifies three patterns and creates one valid original pattern.

# Session 6: Predict Before You Run

## Child promise

Today you will work out what a program should do before pressing Run.

## Story mission

Fara gives Kito a route across a tiled floor.

Route:

1. Move forward two squares.
2. Turn right.
3. Move forward one square.
4. Turn left.
5. Move forward two squares.

Fara asks the learner to point to Kito's finishing square before the route is tested.

## Read: Prediction gives your test a purpose

A prediction is what you expect to happen.

Programmers often read a program and predict its result before running it. This is called tracing.

Tracing means following one instruction at a time and recording what changes.

After the program runs, compare the actual result with your prediction.

If they match, your understanding may be correct.

If they differ, you have discovered something worth investigating.

## Worked example

Kito starts on square A facing right.

Instructions:

1. Move forward one square.
2. Move forward one square.
3. Turn left.
4. Move forward one square.

Trace:

- Start: A, facing right
- After step 1: B, facing right
- After step 2: C, facing right
- After step 3: C, facing up
- After step 4: the square above C, facing up

## Your turn: Where will Fara stop?

Display a 4 by 4 grid. Fara begins at the bottom-left square facing right.

Instructions:

1. Move forward two squares.
2. Turn left.
3. Move forward two squares.
4. Turn right.
5. Move forward one square.

The learner selects a finishing square before pressing Test.

Correct result:

Fara finishes three columns from the left and three rows above the starting row, facing right.

The interface should animate one instruction at a time after the prediction is submitted.

## Hint ladder

### Hint 1

Use your finger to follow the route one instruction at a time.

### Hint 2

A turn changes direction but does not move the character to a new square.

### Hint 3

Place a small position mark after every move instruction.

## Quick check

### Question 1

What is tracing?

Correct answer: Following a program one instruction at a time to predict or record its result.

### Question 2

Does turnRight move the character forward?

Correct answer: No. It changes the direction only.

### Question 3

Why should you predict before testing?

Correct answer: It gives you an expected result to compare with what actually happens.

## Small creation

Draw a 5 by 5 grid and create a route containing:

- At least five move instructions
- At least two turns
- A marked starting square
- A marked predicted finishing square

Then trace the route and check the prediction.

## Explain it yourself

Complete both lines:

"I predicted..."

"After testing, I noticed..."

## Completion evidence

The learner submits a prediction, traces the route and records whether the actual result matched.

# Session 7: The Missing Step

## Child promise

Today you will treat a mistake as a clue and repair one missing instruction.

## Story mission

Kito's morning program says:

1. Wake up.
2. Put on shoes.
3. Eat breakfast.
4. Leave home.

Kito arrives outside wearing pyjamas.

The program ran exactly as written. The problem is that an important step is missing.

## Read: A bug is evidence

A bug is a mistake that causes a program to behave differently from what you expected.

Debugging does not mean changing many things at once.

A careful debugger asks:

1. What did I expect?
2. What actually happened?
3. Where is the first difference?
4. What is the smallest change I can test?

A wrong result gives you information. It shows where your understanding and the program no longer match.

## Worked example

Expected:

The lamp switches on after the learner presses the button.

Actual:

The learner presses the button, but nothing happens.

Program:

1. Wait for button press.
2. Play a sound.

First difference:

The program plays a sound instead of switching on the lamp.

Small change:

Replace "Play a sound" with "Switch on the lamp."

## Your turn: Repair the morning routine

Original routine:

1. Wake up.
2. Put on shoes.
3. Eat breakfast.
4. Leave home.

Choose the best missing step:

- A. Go back to sleep.
- B. Put on clothes.
- C. Start a game.

Correct answer: B

The learner then chooses where to place it.

Correct position:

After waking up and before putting on shoes.

## Bug workshop

### Bug 1

Expected: A quiz begins when Start is pressed.

Actual: Pressing Start shows the final score.

Smallest repair: Replace the final-score action with the first-question action.

### Bug 2

Expected: Kito carries the bag before leaving.

Actual: Kito leaves and then picks up the bag.

Smallest repair: Move "Pick up the bag" before "Leave."

### Bug 3

Expected: The light turns off after ten seconds.

Actual: The light stays on.

Smallest repair: Add "Turn off the light" after the waiting step.

## Hint ladder

### Hint 1

Say the expected result and the actual result out loud.

### Hint 2

Find the first moment when they become different.

### Hint 3

Change one instruction, then test again.

## Quick check

### Question 1

What is a bug?

Correct answer: A mistake that makes a program behave differently from the expected result.

### Question 2

Why should you change one thing at a time?

Correct answer: You can tell whether that specific change fixed the problem.

### Question 3

What should you do after making a repair?

Correct answer: Test the program again.

## Small creation

Write a five-step everyday routine. Secretly remove one important step.

Then:

- State the expected result.
- State the result produced by the incomplete routine.
- Identify the missing step.
- Put it back in the correct position.

## Explain it yourself

Complete this sentence:

"A wrong result can help me because..."

## Completion evidence

The learner repairs three bugs and records one expected-versus-actual comparison.

# Session 8: Boss Project, Teach a Robot

## Project promise

You will combine everything from Stage 1 to teach a robot an everyday mission.

## Choose a mission

Choose one:

- Get ready for school
- Prepare a simple snack
- Organise football practice
- Guide a robot through a room

A learner may propose another safe everyday mission with similar difficulty.

## Project brief

Your robot cannot guess. Create a plan that another person could follow exactly.

Your finished plan must:

- Include at least eight ordered steps.
- Use precise actions.
- Split one part into a smaller job.
- Identify at least one input and output.
- Include a repeating pattern when one naturally appears.
- Predict the final result before testing.
- Record one bug or unclear step found during testing.
- Show the change made after the test.

## Project screens

### Screen 1: Choose the goal

Prompt:

"What should your robot achieve?"

The learner writes one clear goal.

Example:

"The robot will pack a school bag for Monday."

### Screen 2: Break up the job

Prompt:

"What smaller jobs are hiding inside your mission?"

The learner creates at least three groups.

Example:

- Check the timetable
- Collect learning materials
- Pack and check the bag

### Screen 3: Write the instructions

Prompt:

"Write one action on each card. Your robot will follow the cards from top to bottom."

The interface should allow the learner to add, remove and reorder instruction cards.

### Screen 4: Mark input and output

Prompts:

"What information or action starts the mission?"

"What result should appear at the end?"

### Screen 5: Predict

Prompt:

"What do you expect the robot to complete if every step works?"

The learner records a prediction before testing.

### Screen 6: Test with Robot Mode

The app displays one instruction at a time.

The learner acts as the robot or uses an animated Kito preview.

Robot Mode rules:

- Follow only the written instruction.
- Do not fill in missing information.
- Pause when an instruction is vague.
- Record the first place the plan becomes confusing.

### Screen 7: Repair

Prompts:

- What did you expect?
- What actually happened?
- Which step caused the first difference?
- What one change will you test?

### Screen 8: Present

The learner completes:

"My robot's goal was..."

"The hardest step to explain was..."

"Testing showed me..."

"I improved my plan by..."

## Project hint ladder

### Hint 1

Begin with the result. What should be true when the mission is finished?

### Hint 2

Group steps that belong to the same smaller job.

### Hint 3

Use this instruction pattern: Action + object + exact detail.

### Hint 4

If the test fails, find the first difference between what you expected and what happened.

## Project assessment

Score each area from 0 to 2.

### Clear goal

- 0: The goal is missing.
- 1: The goal exists but is vague.
- 2: The goal clearly describes the final result.

### Ordered instructions

- 0: Fewer than five useful steps.
- 1: At least eight steps, but some are misplaced.
- 2: At least eight steps in an order that can be followed.

### Precision

- 0: Many steps require guessing.
- 1: Some steps are clear.
- 2: Each important step identifies a clear action and object.

### Decomposition

- 0: The job is not divided.
- 1: The job has two smaller parts.
- 2: The job has at least three useful smaller parts.

### Prediction and testing

- 0: No prediction or test is recorded.
- 1: A prediction or test is recorded.
- 2: Both a prediction and test result are recorded.

### Debugging evidence

- 0: No problem or change is recorded.
- 1: A problem is identified.
- 2: A problem, cause and tested change are recorded.

Maximum score: 12

Suggested mastery requirement: 9 out of 12, with at least 1 point in every area.

## Badge award

Award the Fox Planner badge when the learner:

- Completes all seven lessons.
- Scores at least 9 out of 12 on the boss project.
- Explains one change made after testing.
- Submits a finished robot plan.

Badge message:

"You earned the Fox Planner badge. You can turn a large idea into small, testable steps."
